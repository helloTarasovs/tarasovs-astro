<div class="ta-qa-label" id="ta-quick-answer">Quick Answer</div>
<p><strong>Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) is the practice of improving how a brand, product, or source is found, understood, mentioned, and cited in AI-generated answers.</strong> It builds on SEO, but measures visibility inside synthesized answers - not only rankings and clicks.</p>
<p>GEO terminology is not fully standardized. When comparing tools or reports, check the exact definition, denominator, prompt set, platform, location, and test date behind every metric.</p>

<h3>GEO (Generative Engine Optimization)</h3>
<p>The practice of improving the visibility and accurate representation of content or entities in generative-engine responses. GEO can include technical crawlability, content structure, source authority, entity clarity, original evidence, and measurement across a defined set of prompts. The term was formalized in the 2024 ACM paper <em>GEO: Generative Engine Optimization</em>. Learn how Tarasovs applies it in our <a href="/services/geo-ai-search-optimization/">GEO and AI search optimization service</a>.</p>
<h3>AEO (Answer Engine Optimization)</h3>
<p>The practice of making content easy to select, extract, and present as a direct answer. AEO is commonly associated with featured snippets, voice assistants, answer boxes, and question-answer formats. Some marketers use AEO as an umbrella term that also includes generative AI answers.</p>
<h3>AI SEO / LLM SEO</h3>
<p>Informal umbrella labels for work intended to improve visibility in AI-assisted discovery. Depending on the author or platform, they may mean technical accessibility, content optimization, brand monitoring, or the full GEO program. Always check the stated scope rather than assuming a fixed definition.</p>
<h3>Generative Engine / Answer Engine</h3>
<p>A search or assistant experience that synthesizes information into a generated response, often with supporting links. The product may combine a language model, a search index, live retrieval, ranking systems, and safety layers; it is not necessarily a standalone LLM.</p>
<h3>LLM (Large Language Model)</h3>
<p>A model trained to understand and generate language, such as models in the GPT, Claude, and Gemini families. An AI search product usually includes an LLM, but its answer may also depend on live web retrieval, proprietary indexes, tools, personalization, and product settings.</p>
<h2 id="how-ai-answers">How AI Systems Build Answers</h2>
<h3>RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)</h3>
<p>A system design in which relevant information is retrieved from a search index, database, or document collection and supplied to a generative model before it answers. RAG can make answers more current and source-grounded, but not every AI response uses live retrieval.</p>
<h3>Retrieval</h3>
<p>The step in which a system finds candidate documents or passages relevant to a query. Retrieval is different from citation: a system may retrieve a page without linking to it in the final answer, or cite a different source that better supports the claim.</p>
<h3>Grounding</h3>
<p>Connecting an AI answer to external evidence, such as web pages, uploaded files, databases, or search results. A grounded answer is constrained by retrieved evidence, although grounding does not guarantee that every statement will be correct.</p>
<h3>Query Fan-Out</h3>
<p>A technique in which one complex request triggers multiple related searches across subtopics or data sources. Google says AI Overviews and AI Mode may use query fan-out. For GEO, this means a page can earn visibility by answering a specific supporting question even when it does not rank for the user's exact wording. See our guide to <a href="/how-to-appear-in-google-ai-overviews/">appearing in Google AI Overviews</a>.</p>
<h3>Embeddings / Vector Search</h3>
<p>Embeddings represent content as numerical vectors so systems can compare meaning, not only exact keywords. Vector search uses those representations to retrieve semantically relevant passages. It is common in RAG systems, but public AI platforms do not disclose every retrieval or ranking signal they use.</p>
<h3>Model Training vs. Live Retrieval</h3>
<p><strong>Training</strong> changes what a future model may learn from large datasets. <strong>Live retrieval</strong> fetches or searches current information when a user asks a question. Allowing or blocking a training crawler is therefore not the same decision as allowing or blocking a search crawler or user-triggered fetcher.</p>
<h2 id="metrics">Metrics and Measurement</h2>
<h3>Prompt / Query Set</h3>
<p>The fixed collection of questions used to measure AI visibility. A defensible prompt set should represent real buyer intent and document the platform, model or mode, language, location, date, and number of repeated runs. Without a stable prompt set, results from different reporting periods are not comparable.</p>
<h3>Branded vs. Unbranded Prompt</h3>
<p>A <strong>branded prompt</strong> names the company or product and tests factual accuracy, positioning, or reputation. An <strong>unbranded prompt</strong> asks for a category, solution, or recommendation without naming the company and is more useful for measuring discovery against competitors.</p>
<h3>Mention Rate</h3>
<p>The percentage of tested answers in which a brand appears at least once. A report should specify whether the denominator is prompts, individual answer runs, or prompt-platform combinations. Mention Rate measures presence; it does not show whether the brand was linked, recommended, or described positively.</p>
<h3>Citation Rate</h3>
<p>The percentage of tested answers that include at least one source link to a brand's owned domain or a defined source. This is different from Mention Rate: an answer can name a brand without citing its website, or cite a page that discusses the category without naming the brand.</p>
<h3>Share of Model (SoM)</h3>
<p>A family of competitive AI-visibility metrics, not one universally standardized formula. In Tarasovs reporting, SoM starts with the percentage of tracked answers in which a brand appears across a fixed prompt set, then adds context such as prominence, citation, and competitor presence. Some platforms instead calculate a brand's share of all mentions or use weighted scores. The formula must be disclosed before two SoM numbers can be compared. See our <a href="/share-of-model-ai-search-visibility/">Share of Model measurement guide</a>.</p>
<h3>Citation Share</h3>
<p>The percentage of all source citations in a measured answer set that point to a specific domain, page, or brand-owned property. Reports should state whether repeated links count more than once and whether third-party pages mentioning the brand are included.</p>
<h3>Prominence / Position</h3>
<p>How visibly a brand appears inside an answer - for example, first recommendation, later alternative, comparison-table entry, or passing reference. Position is useful context, but AI responses are not stable ranked lists, so it should be measured across repeated runs.</p>
<h3>Sentiment</h3>
<p>The tone or framing attached to a brand mention: positive, neutral, mixed, or negative. Sentiment is most useful when reported with the exact answer excerpt and a consistent scoring rubric; automated sentiment labels alone can miss nuance.</p>
<h3>Answer Accuracy</h3>
<p>The percentage of evaluated brand claims that are correct according to a documented source of truth. Accuracy audits often check services, location, pricing, credentials, policies, and product availability separately from visibility.</p>
<h3>AI Visibility Score</h3>
<p>A composite score that may combine mentions, citations, prominence, sentiment, or accuracy. There is no universal formula, so scores from different tools - or from different methodologies - are not directly comparable.</p>
<h3>AI Referral Traffic</h3>
<p>Website visits attributed to links clicked from AI assistants. It captures only measurable clicks, not zero-click brand exposure or visits whose referrer is missing. Our <a href="/track-ai-assistant-traffic-ga4/">GA4 guide to AI assistant traffic</a> explains how to report it separately.</p>
<h3>Zero-Click Search</h3>
<p>A search or assistant interaction that satisfies the user's need without a website visit. Zero-click behavior makes mentions, citations, and accurate brand representation valuable even when referral traffic remains low.</p>
<h2 id="technical-terms">Technical Terms</h2>
<h3>AI Crawlers and User-Triggered Fetchers</h3>
<p>Automated agents that access web content for different purposes. They should not be treated as one interchangeable group:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Search and discovery:</strong> OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, and Claude-SearchBot help their products surface or improve web results.</li>
<li><strong>User-triggered retrieval:</strong> ChatGPT-User, Perplexity-User, and Claude-User may fetch a page in response to a person's request.</li>
<li><strong>Model development:</strong> GPTBot and ClaudeBot are associated with content that may be used to improve future models.</li>
</ul>
<p>Names and functions can change, so production access rules should be checked against each provider's current documentation.</p>
<h3>robots.txt</h3>
<p>A file that communicates crawl preferences to compliant automated agents. Rules apply to the named user agent, which is why blocking GPTBot does not automatically block OAI-SearchBot. A robots.txt rule is not access control: private content still requires authentication or another security layer.</p>
<h3>Google-Extended</h3>
<p>A robots.txt product token - not a separate HTTP crawler - that controls certain uses of Google-crawled content for Gemini model development and grounding. It does not determine eligibility for Google Search features such as AI Overviews, which rely on Google's Search systems and controls.</p>
<h3><code>llms.txt</code></h3>
<p>A proposed Markdown file that gives AI agents a curated map of a website's most useful content. It is best treated as an optional navigation layer - not a ranking factor, crawler permission file, or guaranteed citation tactic. See the <a href="/llms-txt-guide/">complete llms.txt implementation guide</a>.</p>
<h3><code>llms-full.txt</code></h3>
<p>A companion convention that consolidates larger amounts of page content into one LLM-readable file. It can help a known consumer ingest documentation or a knowledge base, but it creates duplication and freshness risks. Build it only when there is a clear use case and maintenance process.</p>
<h3><code>AGENTS.md</code></h3>
<p>A repository instruction file used by coding agents to understand project-specific commands, conventions, and constraints. It is not an alternative to <code>llms.txt</code> for general website discovery and is not a known AI search ranking signal.</p>
<h3>Structured Data / Schema Markup</h3>
<p>Machine-readable markup - commonly JSON-LD - that describes entities and page content using a shared vocabulary such as Schema.org. Google uses supported structured data to understand pages and enable eligible search features. Schema can reduce ambiguity, but it does not guarantee ranking, extraction, or citation across AI platforms.</p>
<h3>Semantic HTML</h3>
<p>HTML that uses meaningful elements and a logical heading structure to communicate the role of content. Clear headings, lists, tables, navigation, and article sections make pages easier for browsers, assistive technology, search engines, and retrieval systems to parse.</p>
<h2 id="content-authority">Content and Authority</h2>
<h3>Entity</h3>
<p>A uniquely identifiable person, organization, product, place, or concept. Entity clarity means keeping names, relationships, services, locations, and key facts consistent enough that systems can connect information to the correct subject.</p>
<h3>Entity Resolution</h3>
<p>The process of determining whether references from different pages or datasets describe the same entity. Consistent naming and corroborating facts help distinguish a brand from similarly named companies, locations, or products.</p>
<h3>Knowledge Graph</h3>
<p>A structured network of entities and the relationships between them. Search engines and other information systems use knowledge graphs to connect facts, disambiguate names, and support richer answers.</p>
<h3>E-E-A-T (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness)</h3>
<p>A framework used in Google's search quality guidance. E-E-A-T is not a single ranking factor or a universal score shared by every AI platform. For GEO, the practical lesson is to make experience, authorship, evidence, reputation, and accountability visible and verifiable.</p>
<h3>Citation vs. Mention vs. Recommendation</h3>
<p>A <strong>citation</strong> links or attributes information to a source. A <strong>mention</strong> names a brand without necessarily linking to it. A <strong>recommendation</strong> actively presents the brand as a suitable choice. Reports should separate all three because they represent different levels of visibility and influence.</p>
<h3>Source of Truth</h3>
<p>The authoritative page or system that owns a specific fact, such as an address, price, credential, service name, or policy. Other pages should remain consistent with it and link back where appropriate to reduce conflicting information.</p>
<h3>Third-Party Corroboration</h3>
<p>Independent sources that confirm a brand claim - for example, official registries, reputable directories, professional profiles, press coverage, reviews, and partner pages. Corroboration makes claims easier to verify, but the quality and relevance of sources matter more than raw mention volume.</p>
<h3>Original / First-Party Data</h3>
<p>Research, measurements, benchmarks, case-study results, or observations produced directly by the publisher. Clear methodology, dates, sample sizes, and limitations make first-party data more trustworthy and more useful as a citable source.</p>
<h3>Hallucination</h3>
<p>An AI-generated statement that is unsupported, fabricated, or factually wrong. Contradictory or outdated web information can make accurate retrieval harder, but even well-structured content cannot eliminate hallucinations. Important brand facts should be monitored and corrected at their authoritative sources.</p>
</div>

<h2 id="ta-faq-title">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<div class="ta-faq-list">
<details class="ta-faq-item">
<summary class="ta-faq-question">Is GEO the same as SEO or AEO?</summary>
<p class="ta-faq-answer">They overlap, but the measurement focus differs. SEO usually measures visibility and traffic from ranked results. AEO measures selection as a direct answer. GEO measures whether a source or brand is accurately included, cited, or recommended in a generated response. Some companies use AEO, GEO, and AI SEO interchangeably, so always check the methodology.</p>
</details>
<details class="ta-faq-item">
<summary class="ta-faq-question">Is GEO terminology standardized?</summary>
<p class="ta-faq-answer">No. Core concepts such as retrieval, grounding, citation, and RAG have established technical meanings, but marketing metrics such as Share of Model and AI Visibility Score still vary by platform. A credible report states its formula, denominator, prompt set, platforms, dates, and repeat-run method.</p>
</details>
<details class="ta-faq-item">
<summary class="ta-faq-question">Does an <code>llms.txt</code> file improve AI rankings?</summary>
<p class="ta-faq-answer">There is no reliable evidence that publishing <code>llms.txt</code> independently improves search rankings or AI citations. It can provide a useful, curated navigation layer when an agent chooses to read it, but it does not replace crawlability, indexability, clear content, authority, or third-party evidence.</p>
</details>
<details class="ta-faq-item">
<summary class="ta-faq-question">Does allowing GPTBot make a site appear in ChatGPT Search?</summary>
<p class="ta-faq-answer">Not by itself. OpenAI documents GPTBot for potential model-development use and OAI-SearchBot for surfacing websites in ChatGPT search experiences. Their robots.txt controls are separate. User-triggered page visits may also use ChatGPT-User.</p>
</details>
<details class="ta-faq-item">
<summary class="ta-faq-question">Which GEO metric should a business track first?</summary>
<p class="ta-faq-answer">Start with Mention Rate across a fixed set of high-intent, mostly unbranded prompts. Then separate owned-domain citations, recommendation prominence, sentiment, factual accuracy, and competitor presence. A single composite score is useful only when its formula remains transparent.</p>
</details>
<details class="ta-faq-item">
<summary class="ta-faq-question">How often should AI visibility be measured?</summary>
<p class="ta-faq-answer">Monthly measurement is a practical baseline for most businesses. Use the same prompt set and document platform, model or mode, language, location, date, and repeated runs. Re-test after major content, technical, product, or reputation changes, but avoid treating one answer as a stable ranking.</p>
</details>
<details class="ta-faq-item">
<summary class="ta-faq-question">Can GEO guarantee citations or recommendations?</summary>
<p class="ta-faq-answer">No. AI answers vary by platform, retrieval source, model version, location, personalization, and time. GEO can improve source eligibility, clarity, authority, and measurement, but no credible provider can guarantee that a third-party AI system will cite or recommend a brand.</p>
</details>
