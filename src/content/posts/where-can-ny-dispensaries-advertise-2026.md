<div class="ta-qa__label">Quick Answer</div>
<p><strong>No. Licensed cannabis dispensaries cannot advertise on Google Ads, and they cannot advertise on Facebook or Instagram either.</strong> Both platforms prohibit cannabis and THC product promotion outright, and a state license does not create an exception under either policy. New York adds its own layer on top: Part 129 bans billboards, public transit advertising, vehicle signage, and any advertising on publicly owned property.</p>
<p>New York's marketing rules changed on <strong>3 December 2025</strong>. The biggest practical change is that discounts, sales, and loyalty programs are now explicitly permitted, subject to a price floor. Many guides published before that date still say discounts are banned. They are out of date.</p>
<p>What follows is what is actually left, and one technical trap that catches a lot of dispensary websites: Part 129 requires an age gate on your site, and a badly built age gate can remove almost every page you have from Google.</p>

<h2 id="google">Can dispensaries advertise on Google Ads?</h2>
<p>No, and the reason is worth understanding because it explains why there is no workaround to find.</p>
<p>Google's <a href="https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16489299" rel="noopener">Recreational drugs policy</a>, part of its broader Dangerous Products or Services policy, prohibits ads for substances that alter mental state for the purpose of recreation, and it names marijuana explicitly. The same policy also bans ads for products marketed as facilitating recreational drug use, listing pipes, bongs, and cannabis coffee shops as examples.</p>
<p>This is Google's global platform policy. State licensing does not create an exception under it, so a fully licensed dispensary in Manhattan is treated the same as any other cannabis advertiser. Google states the prohibition without spelling out its reasoning, so it is worth being careful about asserting motive on Google's behalf. What matters commercially is the outcome, which is unambiguous.</p>
<h3>A note on federal scheduling, because it has changed</h3>
<p>You may have seen headlines about cannabis rescheduling and wondered whether the platform position is about to shift. The status as of August 2026 is narrower than the headlines suggest.</p>
<p>On 23 April 2026 the Justice Department and DEA <a href="https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-places-fda-approved-marijuana-products-and-products-containing-marijuana" rel="noopener">issued an order</a> placing two categories into Schedule III: FDA-approved products containing marijuana, and marijuana products regulated under a qualifying state medical marijuana license. Broader rescheduling of marijuana generally from Schedule I to Schedule III remains in an administrative process, with a DEA hearing that began on 29 June 2026.</p>
<p>Neither category covers adult-use retail. Cannabis sold by a New York adult-use dispensary remains federally prohibited and does not fall within Google's permitted CBD categories. If the broader rescheduling completes, platform policies may or may not follow, and that is worth watching. It has not happened yet.</p>
<h3>The exceptions, and why they will not help a dispensary</h3>
<p>There are exceptions, but none of them apply to a THC retailer:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Topical hemp-derived CBD</strong> with THC content of 0.3% or less is allowed. Google gives creams, sprays, roll-ons, lotions, bath bombs, and candles as examples.</li>
<li><strong>FDA-approved pharmaceutical CBD</strong> advertisers must apply for certification through LegitScript and may only target California, Colorado, and Puerto Rico.</li>
<li><strong>A limited pilot program in Canada</strong> for certain legally permitted cannabis product types.</li>
</ul>
<p>Notice what is missing from that list: adult-use THC retail, anywhere in the United States, in any state.</p>
<div class="ta-callout">
<strong>One useful detail if you have been running ads you should not have been.</strong> Google states that violations of the recreational drugs policy will not lead to immediate account suspension without prior warning, and that a warning is issued at least seven days before any suspension. That is a narrow grace period, not a loophole. Repeated violations are what get accounts permanently suspended.
</div>
<h2 id="platforms">Meta, and the rest of the paid landscape</h2>
<p>Meta's advertising standards prohibit ads that promote or offer for sale THC products or cannabis products containing related psychoactive components, and the ban explicitly covers both ingestible and non-ingestible products. As with Google, there is a narrow exception for CBD at or below 0.3% THC, which requires both LegitScript certification and written authorisation from Meta, may only target the United States, and may not target anyone under 18 or make health claims.</p>
<p>One detail worth noting if you sell accessories: Meta also bans ads promoting the sale of drug paraphernalia, naming bongs, rolling papers, and vapes. Google's policy does the same, listing pipes and bongs. So the fallback plan of advertising the accessories rather than the flower does not work either.</p>
<p>So the two platforms that between them absorb most local advertising budgets in almost every other retail category are both closed to you. This is the single most important structural fact about marketing a dispensary, and it shapes everything else in this guide.</p>
<h2 id="banned">What New York bans outright</h2>
<p>Federal-driven platform bans are only half the picture. New York's Part 129 regulations, administered by the <a href="https://cannabis.ny.gov/part-129-guidance" rel="noopener">Office of Cannabis Management</a>, close off most of what remains offline.</p>
<div class="ta-ban">
<div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Billboards</span><span class="ta-ban__what">Prohibited entirely. New York defines a billboard precisely by size and distance, which matters more than most operators realise. See below.</span></div>
<div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Public transit</span><span class="ta-ban__what">No advertising in or on public transit vehicles or stations.</span></div>
<div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Public property</span><span class="ta-ban__what">No advertising on publicly owned or operated property.</span></div>
<div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Vehicles</span><span class="ta-ban__what">Licensees cannot use vehicles to advertise at all. The only exception is vehicle markings required to comply with other laws. No wraps, no branded delivery vans.</span></div>
<div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Within 500 feet</span><span class="ta-ban__what">No advertising within or readily observed within 500 feet of the perimeter of a school ground, playground, child day care provider, public park, or library.</span></div>
<div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Certain venues</span><span class="ta-ban__what">No signs in arenas, stadiums, sporting venues, state-funded fairs, video game arcades, or shopping malls. <div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Youth appeal</span><span class="ta-ban__what">No cartoons, no mascots, no candy language or spelling variants, no imagery associated with children's products. <div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Potency as a selling point</span><span class="ta-ban__what">You may state THC concentration, but you cannot promote potency or imply a product is better because it is stronger. <div class="ta-ban__item"><span class="ta-ban__name">Health and wellness claims</span><span class="ta-ban__what">No curative or therapeutic claims, no medical symbols, and nothing that blurs the line between adult-use, medical cannabis, and hemp CBD.</span></div>

<h3>How New York actually defines a billboard</h3>
<p>This is one of the most commonly misunderstood parts of Part 129, because "no billboards" sounds simple until you try to work out whether your storefront sign counts.</p>
<p>A sign becomes a prohibited billboard if it exceeds:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>55 square feet</strong> when situated within a 100 yard radius of the licensed premises, a permitted showcase event, or another event the licensee is sponsoring, or</li>
<li><strong>16 square feet</strong> when situated more than 100 yards from the entrance to the licensed premises or event.</li>
</ul>
<p>In other words, the further a sign sits from your store, the smaller it is allowed to be before it is legally a billboard. And what you are permitted to put on an outdoor sign is a short, closed list: your licensee or DBA name, your dispensary address, your phone number, email address and website, directions to the business, and the licensed activity. Product imagery, offers, and slogans do not belong there.</p>
<h2 id="changed">What changed on 3 December 2025</h2>
<p>The Cannabis Control Board approved amendments to the Packaging, Labeling, Marketing and Advertising regulations that took effect on 3 December 2025. Most provisions were effective immediately, with a six-month delayed compliance window for certain packaging and labeling requirements that closed on <strong>3 June 2026</strong>. Both dates have now passed, so the amended rules are fully in force.</p>
<p>If you are reading an article about New York cannabis marketing that was published before December 2025 and has not been updated, assume it is wrong on the following points.</p>
<h3>Discounts and loyalty programs are now permitted</h3>
<p>This is the change that matters most commercially. Retail licensees may now run promotional pricing: limited-time percentage discounts, volume-based discounts such as buy three save ten percent, event-based promotions such as a 4/20 weekend sale, and loyalty programs that award points toward future discounts.</p>
<p>The constraint is a price floor. Market value is defined as the minimum retail price of a product, calculated as <strong>1.5 times the wholesale price</strong> the retailer paid for the specific product or units being discounted. You cannot discount below that, and you cannot stack discounts in a way that takes the effective price below it.</p>
<p>Still prohibited: giving cannabis away free, buy-one-get-one-free offers, free samples at the register, birthday freebies that require no purchase, and gamified promotions such as spin-the-wheel or scratch-off mechanisms, which are treated as potentially attractive to under-21s. Retail tax must be calculated on the pre-discount price.</p>
<h3>Where you can advertise those promotions</h3>
<p>This is the part that quietly makes the case for owning your own channels. Promotional pricing can be advertised freely <em>inside</em> your licensed premises. Outside the premises, it may only be advertised through four channels:</p>
<ul>
<li>Electronic communications such as email and SMS</li>
<li>Mailers</li>
<li><strong>The licensee's official website</strong></li>
<li>A third-party website allowed by the licensee where the audience is limited to adults 21 and over</li>
</ul>
<p>That is the entire list. Your own website is one of only four legal places to advertise a sale to someone who is not already standing in your store.</p>
<h3>Outdoor signage limits loosened</h3>
<p>The amendments also removed the previous limit of two outdoor signs per licensee and drew clearer lines between permitted outdoor signage and prohibited billboards. Retail dispensaries can now use additional outdoor signage, within the size and content rules described above.</p>
<h2 id="required">What every compliant ad must contain</h2>
<p>The regulation gives you a choice of delivery that a lot of summaries miss. For advertising with visual elements, or a combination of audio and visual elements, the required warnings may be <strong>either displayed conspicuously on the face of the ad or clearly read aloud</strong> at the same volume, pace, and language as the rest of the ad. The typography rules below apply only when the warning is printed or otherwise written.</p>
<div class="ta-rule">
<h3 class="ta-rule__name">1. Your license information</h3>
<p>Your name and license number, accurate and legible, in all advertising and advertisements, unless the form has been otherwise approved by the Office. This does not apply to brand representatives or cannabis merchandise, though a license number must be provided on request when using a brand representative.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-rule">
<h3 class="ta-rule__name">2. The required warning statement</h3>
<p>For ads with visual elements, or audio and visual elements combined, displayed or read aloud, verbatim:</p>
<div class="ta-warn">For use only by persons 21 years of age and older. Keep out of reach of children and pets. If someone accidentally consumes cannabis, contact the Poison Center. Consume responsibly.</div>
<p class="ta-rule__cite">Audio-only advertising uses a different, shorter statement, read aloud: For persons 21 and older. Keep away from children and pets. If cannabis is accidentally consumed, contact the Poison Center.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-rule">
<h3 class="ta-rule__name">3. One rotating warning, genuinely rotated</h3>
<p>Required for everything except audio-only advertising, brand representatives, and cannabis merchandise. Again, displayed conspicuously or read aloud. One of these four, or another authorised by the Office:</p>
<ul>
<li>Cannabis can be addictive.</li>
<li>Cannabis can impair concentration and coordination. Do not operate a vehicle or machinery under the influence of cannabis.</li>
<li>There may be health risks associated with consumption of this product.</li>
<li>Cannabis is not recommended for use by persons who are pregnant or nursing.</li>
</ul>
<p>The regulation requires rotation as directed by the Office. OCM's guidance expects the warnings to be distributed roughly evenly, either across the ads you run in a year or across the number of people who see them.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-rule">
<h3 class="ta-rule__name">4. HOPEline information, and this one is cumulative</h3>
<p>This is the requirement most often summarised incorrectly, including in earlier versions of this article. The regulation requires the New York State HOPEline <strong>phone number, text number, and website or QR code</strong>. The first two are both mandatory. The only choice is between the website URL and a QR code linking to it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Phone:</strong> 1-877-8-HOPENY, also written 1-877-846-7369</li>
<li><strong>Text:</strong> HOPENY, also written 467369</li>
<li><strong>Website or QR code:</strong> oasas.ny.gov/hopeline, or a QR code linking directly to it</li>
</ul>
<p>Brand representatives and cannabis merchandise are excepted, unless the Office exempts other formats.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-rule">
<h3 class="ta-rule__name">5. The yellow box, when the warning is printed</h3>
<p>If the warnings are printed or otherwise written on the ad, the regulation requires them to appear in a <strong>bright yellow text box</strong> so they stand out, in Times New Roman, Calibri, Arial, or Helvetica, at no smaller than 6-point, easily legible, contrasting from the background, unobscured, and in the same language as the ad. Where the surrounding ad is itself yellow, the box must be offset with a distinctive border.</p>
<p>OCM's guidance goes further than the regulation text and specifies the shade as hex <strong>#FFFF00</strong>, noting that a visually consistent similar shade is acceptable but that saturation below or above 75% will not be. Your license number and the HOPEline information are not required to sit inside the yellow box.</p>
</div>
<h2 id="online">Online and social media rules</h2>
<p>Three requirements here matter more than the rest, and the first one is the one that leads directly into the technical problem in the next section.</p>
<h3>Your website must have an age-restriction mechanism</h3>
<p>When advertising through a website or digital application, a licensee must use an age-restriction mechanism designed to require a visitor to verify they are at least 21. This is not optional, and it is worth remembering that under Part 129 your menu counts as an advertisement.</p>
<h3>No unsolicited pop-up or banner advertising</h3>
<p>If your advertisement is a pop-up or banner on a website or app, the person viewing it must consent to view cannabis-related material before it can be displayed. Unsolicited pop-up or banner advertising is prohibited except on age-restricted sites and apps serving people 21 and over who have consented to see cannabis content.</p>
<h3>Social profiles need a 21+ notice and your license number</h3>
<p>Marketing on any social platform must display, clearly visible on the profile page itself, text stating that cannabis products are legal only for adults 21 and older, together with your license or permit number. OCM gives worked examples of acceptable bio text, such as <code>21+ only. OCM-RETL-XX-XXXXXX</code> or <code>Sales restricted to adults 21 and over. OCM-CAURD-XX-XXXXXX</code>.</p>
<p>Licensees are also required to disseminate social advertising only to user accounts where the user has verified they are 21 or older. In practice this constrains organic social reach considerably, which again pushes weight back onto owned and organic channels.</p>
<h2 id="agegate">The age gate trap</h2>
<p>Here is where cannabis compliance and search visibility collide.</p>
<p>Part 129 requires an age-restriction mechanism on your website. Meanwhile, Google's own <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/avoid-intrusive-interstitials" rel="noopener">documentation on interstitials and dialogs</a> explains what age gates do to crawling and indexing. Both things are true simultaneously, and the intersection is easy to get wrong, because the people who understand the regulation are usually lawyers and the people who understand indexing are usually not reading cannabis regulations.</p>
<h3>The mistake that deletes your site from Google</h3>
<p>Google's guidance is unusually blunt on this point. On mandatory interstitials it says not to redirect incoming HTTP requests to a different page for collecting consent, because <strong>redirecting all URLs to a single page will remove all but that one page from search results, as Googlebot can only fetch that page.</strong></p>
<p>That is the single most damaging implementation, and it is a common configuration error rather than an exotic one. A dispensary installs an age gate plugin, the plugin redirects every incoming visitor to <code>/age-verification/</code>, and over the following weeks the site's product pages, category pages, location pages, and blog posts fall out of the index one by one. The store still looks fine to anyone who arrives directly. It is simply no longer findable.</p>
<p>Because Googlebot does not click buttons or fill in forms, any gate that must be interacted with before content loads can prevent that content from being crawled at all.</p>
<h3>What Google actually recommends instead</h3>
<p>Google's documented recommendations for mandatory age gates are specific:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Overlay, do not replace.</strong> Ensure the content is overlaid with the interstitial rather than substituted for it, so that Google can still index at least some of the content and potentially show it in search results.</li>
<li><strong>Do not redirect.</strong> Keep each URL serving its own content rather than sending all requests to one consent page.</li>
<li><strong>Consider serving Googlebot without the gate.</strong> In Google's words, for content behind a mandatory age gate for adult users they recommend allowing Googlebot to crawl the content without triggering the age gate, by verifying Googlebot requests and serving the content without the gate.</li>
</ul>
<div class="ta-callout">
<strong>On the cloaking question.</strong> Serving different content to search engines than to users is normally a spam policy violation. This case is the documented exception: Google explicitly recommends it for mandatory age gates. If you do it, verify Googlebot properly through reverse DNS lookup rather than trusting the user-agent string, which anyone can spoof. Verifying by user-agent alone means anyone claiming to be Googlebot walks straight past your age gate, which defeats the compliance purpose entirely.
</div>
<h3>The honest caveat</h3>
<p>We are a search agency, not a law firm, and there is a question here we cannot answer for you. Part 129 requires a mechanism that requires a <em>visitor</em> to verify their age. Whether serving an automated crawler without that gate satisfies the regulation is a question for your compliance counsel, not for an SEO. What we can tell you with certainty is the technical half: the redirect-everything implementation is catastrophic for search visibility, the overlay implementation is not, and the difference between the two is usually a configuration choice nobody consciously made.</p>
<p>If your dispensary also runs an embedded menu, this problem compounds with the one we covered in <a href="/dutchie-menu-seo/">Dutchie menu SEO</a>: an iframe-embedded catalogue is already largely invisible to Google, and an age gate layered on top of it can make the rest of the site invisible too.</p>

<h2 id="faq">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<div class="ta-faq">
<div class="ta-faq__list">
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">Can a licensed dispensary run Google Ads in New York?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">No. Google's recreational drugs policy prohibits ads for marijuana and for products marketed as facilitating recreational drug use. It is a global platform policy, and a New York State license does not create an exception under it. The only cannabis-adjacent exceptions Google allows are topical hemp-derived CBD at or below 0.3% THC, FDA-approved pharmaceutical CBD limited to California, Colorado and Puerto Rico, and a limited pilot program in Canada.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">Can dispensaries advertise on Facebook or Instagram?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">No. Meta prohibits ads promoting THC products or cannabis products containing related psychoactive components, covering both ingestible and non-ingestible products. There is a narrow exception for CBD products at or below 0.3% THC, which requires written approval from Meta and LegitScript certification. Organic posting is a separate question from paid advertising, and is subject to New York's own social media rules.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">Are discounts and sales allowed for New York dispensaries?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">Yes, as of the amendments effective 3 December 2025. Retail licensees may run limited-time percentage discounts, volume discounts, event-based promotions and loyalty programs. The constraint is a price floor: no product may be discounted below market value, defined as 1.5 times the wholesale price the retailer paid. Free product, buy-one-get-one-free offers and gamified promotions such as spin-the-wheel remain prohibited, and retail tax must be calculated on the pre-discount price.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">Where can a dispensary advertise a sale outside its store?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">Only through four channels: electronic communications such as email and SMS, mailers, the licensee's own official website, and a third-party website allowed by the licensee where the audience is limited to adults 21 and over. Promotional pricing may be advertised freely inside the licensed premises, including on in-store menus.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">Does New York allow cannabis billboards?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">No. Part 129 prohibits billboards for cannabis or cannabis services. A sign is legally a billboard if it exceeds 55 square feet when situated within a 100 yard radius of the licensed premises or sponsored event, or exceeds 16 square feet when situated more than 100 yards away. Permitted outdoor signs are limited in content to the licensee name, dispensary address, phone number, email, website, directions, and the licensed activity.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">What warning text is required on a New York cannabis advertisement?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">Advertising with visual elements, or combined audio and visual elements, must carry this statement either displayed conspicuously on the face of the ad or clearly read aloud: For use only by persons 21 years of age and older. Keep out of reach of children and pets. If someone accidentally consumes cannabis, contact the Poison Center. Consume responsibly. Audio-only advertising uses a shorter statement read aloud. Most formats must also carry one of four rotating warnings, plus HOPEline information and the licensee name and license number. When the warnings are printed rather than read aloud, they must appear in a bright yellow text box in Times New Roman, Calibri, Arial or Helvetica at no smaller than 6-point type.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">Do I need to include all of the HOPEline contact methods, or can I pick one?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">The phone number and the text number are both required, and you additionally need either the website or a QR code linking to it. Part 129 requires the New York State HOPEline phone number, text number, and website or QR code, so the only choice is between the URL and the QR code. The phone number is 1-877-8-HOPENY, also written 1-877-846-7369, the text keyword is HOPENY, also written 467369, and the website is oasas.ny.gov/hopeline. Brand representatives and cannabis merchandise are excepted.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">Does my dispensary website need an age gate?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">Yes. When advertising through a website or digital application, Part 129 requires an age-restriction mechanism designed to require a visitor to verify they are at least 21 years old. Under Part 129 a menu counts as an advertisement, so this applies to the menu as well as the rest of the site.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">Can an age gate hurt my Google rankings?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">It can remove pages from Google entirely if implemented badly. Google's documentation states that redirecting all URLs to a single consent page will remove all but that one page from search results, because Googlebot can only fetch that page. Google recommends overlaying the interstitial on content that has already loaded rather than replacing it, not redirecting incoming requests to a separate page, and considering serving verified Googlebot requests without triggering the age gate.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">What audience composition threshold applies to cannabis ads in New York?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">A licensee may only advertise if the audience is reasonably expected to consist of a proportion of people aged 21 and older that meets or exceeds the proportion of New York State's population aged 21 and older, based on the most recent U.S. Census Bureau estimates. This applies to advertising generally, with one exception: advertising that can only be viewed or heard solely from within the licensed premises. OCM's worked example using 2023 Census data puts the threshold at 75.9%, but the figure changes as Census data updates, and the burden of proving audience composition sits with the licensee.</p>
</div>
<div class="ta-faq__item">
<h3 class="ta-faq__q">What happens if a dispensary breaks the advertising rules?</h3>
<p class="ta-faq__a">Licensees must immediately remove or discontinue any advertising the Office determines violates the Cannabis Law or Part 129, and the same applies if a licensee fails to produce compliance records on request. The Office may recommend suspension, cancellation or revocation of the license to the Board, impose fines or fees, order non-compliant advertising to stop by a set date, and impose other penalties authorised under the Cannabis Law.</p>
